## Working papers

### A Taxing Dilemma: Land Take, Fiscal Revenues and Municipal Budgets
<details>
  <summary>Abstract</summary>
  <div style="font-size: 0.9em; margin-bottom: 1em;">
    Urban expansion contributes to biodiversity loss and raises travel costs without increasing housing production. Yet land-related taxes make up a large share of municipal budgets, which may push local authorities to develop land when revenue falls short, a taxing dilemma between preserving soils and raising revenue. Using a panel of France's land-related taxes and quasi-experimental variation in exposure to toxic loans, I analyse how financial constraints affect land take. After the 2015 Swiss franc unpegging quadrupled their interest charges, exposed municipalities refinanced into larger, longer-term debt and left tax rates and spending unchanged. Land take did not respond: I rule out effects larger than 0.39% of the stock of artificialised land, with no heterogeneity across several dimensions. Land take explains only 16.5% of property tax revenue growth, far less than the national revaluation of cadastral values, and most new housing floor area requires no new land. Converting soil is a weak revenue lever.
  </div>
</details>

### Land-Use Litigation and the Microgeography of NIMBYism
<p style="font-size: 0.85em; margin-top: 0.2em; margin-bottom: 0.5em;"><em>with <a href="https://sites.google.com/view/francoislafont11/accueil">François Lafont</a></em></p>

<details>
  <summary>Abstract</summary>
  <div style="font-size: 0.9em; margin-bottom: 1em;">
    As cities grow, building in already populated areas becomes central to housing affordability and land preservation, but also triggers not-in-my-backyard (NIMBY) opposition. To measure it, we construct a dataset from judicial archives covering all construction and land-use litigation in France since 2005. Using an LLM, we extract with high accuracy rich contextual information from over 200,000 court decisions. We document that NIMBY opposition primarily takes the form of individuals challenging housing permits, disproportionately targeting multifamily housing. Consistent with a simple model in which neighbors litigate to protect their property values, appeals to approved housing concentrate in dense and expensive areas. Second-home owners also account for a significant share of litigation, especially in high-end tourist locations. In low-density areas, by contrast, litigation more often comes from owners of undeveloped land appealing permit denials to preserve their building rights.
  </div>
</details>

### Distributional Analysis of Land Take: Human and Ecological Impacts in France
<p style="font-size: 0.85em; margin-top: 0.2em; margin-bottom: 0.5em;"><em>with <a href="https://cesco.mnhn.fr/fr/annuaire/harold-levrel-6495">Harold Levrel</a></em></p>

<details>
  <summary>Abstract</summary>
  <div style="font-size: 0.9em; margin-bottom: 1em;">
    In France, land take disproportionately affects agricultural land, with land prices surging up to 65 times upon reclassification as buildable. This process not only threatens biodiversity but also exacerbates socio-economic inequalities, as wealth disparities increasingly limit access to homeownership, especially for younger generations. This research project investigates the relationship between land tenure inequalities and artificialization, focusing on the distribution of ownership and the beneficiaries of associated land rents. By constructing a comprehensive national database of real estate property, including socio-economic characteristics of owners, we analyze ownership concentration before and after land take.
  </div>
</details>
